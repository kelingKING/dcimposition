Dieline line types

Each line type is typically kept on its own spot-colour / non-printing layer so RIP and cutting software can separate tool paths.

Line styleTypeWhat it means
Cut Full separation through the material. Own spot colour, commonly named CutContour or Dieline, set to overprint so it never knocks out or prints.
Crease / score No material removed - a rule or matrix impression that lets board fold cleanly along the line. Thicker board often needs a counter-score on the reverse to stop the outer face cracking.
Perforation Alternating cut and tie (bridge) segments, defined by a cut:tie ratio (e.g. 2mm cut / 1mm tie). Longer ties = harder to tear but stronger; shorter ties = easier tear-off but risk of premature separation in transit.
Drill / punch Round holes at specified diameter and X/Y position, for hang tabs, binding, or fitments. Kept as its own layer since it often runs on a different tool/bit than the cutting blade.
Glue area Not a cutting element - a masked zone marking where adhesive is applied, usually on its own non-printing layer. Must be kept clear of ink, varnish, and coatings, since most coatings act as release agents and stop the glue bonding, causing seam failure on the finished carton.
Convention varies by shop/CAD package, but the pattern is consistent: cut = solid, crease = dashed, perf = dash-with-ties, drill = discrete points, glue = a filled/hatched zone rather than a line.
All dieline elements must be set to overprint - cut, crease, perforation, and drill alike. If any of these sit as knockout instead of overprint, they'll punch a gap through the artwork underneath wherever they cross it, and on a RIP or platesetter that doesn't recognise the spot colour as non-printing, the line can output as a visible printed mark. Overprint keeps the artwork intact underneath and keeps the dieline purely informational for the press/cutter.

Label / sticker dieline: cut-to vs cut-through

Blade depth is the deciding factor - how deep into the material stack the cut travels.

Kiss-cut (cut-to)
Face material
Adhesive
Liner
Cut stops at the top of the liner - label peels off, liner stays whole as a carrier. What most standalone label/sticker jobs need.
Through-cut
Face material
Adhesive
Liner
Cut goes through every layer including the liner - full separation, no shared carrier. Used for loose singulated labels or shapes that need to drop free.
These are usually two separate spot-colour/layer names in the dieline file so the RIP or cutter can apply different blade depths per pass. Mislabeling one as the other is a common cause of either the liner shattering (over-cut) or the label refusing to peel (under-cut).

Conventional cut vs digicut

Same dieline shape, two different ways to actually cut it - the choice is mostly driven by run length and how much lead time the job can afford.

Conventional die-cuttingDigital cutting (digicut)
Role of the dieline in the file Just a reference - it shows where the cut will fall for placement, sign-off, and for the die-maker to build the physical tool from. The actual cutting is done by that separate die, so line style/spot-colour naming on file matters less to the cut itself. It is the toolpath - the cutter reads that exact vector and the knife or laser follows it directly. No separate tool exists, so the path has to be clean, closed, and correctly named/isolated or the machine cuts it wrong.
Tooling Physical steel rule die (or rotary cylinder die) built to the exact dieline shape - blades bent and set into a plywood or steel base, with separate rule for crease/perf. No physical die - a knife or laser head follows the dieline vector directly from the digital file on a flatbed table (e.g. Zund, Kongsberg, Esko Kappa).
Lead time Die-making takes days, longer for complex or multi-up shapes. Any shape change means a new or reworked die. Effectively none - upload the file and cut. Shape changes are just a file edit.
Cost structure High upfront tooling cost, very low cost per unit at volume. Die is reusable indefinitely if stored and maintained. No tooling cost, but higher cost per unit - table time is the expense, and it scales with run length.
Best for Long runs and repeat jobs - commercial packaging, folding cartons, high-volume label converting. Short runs, prototypes, sign-off proofs, one-offs, and variable or personalised shapes.
Speed at volume Fast - a press-mounted die cuts a full sheet or web in one stroke or continuous rotary pass. Slower per piece - the head traces the outline, so throughput drops as complexity or quantity rises.
Crease / perf Built into the same die - matched creasing rule and perforating rule sit alongside the cutting rule in one pass. Handled with a scoring wheel or matrix tool on the same table, swapped in per pass - fully programmable, no rule-bending required.
Repeatability Extremely consistent shape-to-shape once the die is proven in, since every cut comes from the same physical tool. Consistent too, but tool wear (blade dulling) and material shifting on the table are the main sources of drift to watch.
Rule of thumb: if the die pays for itself before the job ends, go conventional. If the job is a proof, a prototype, or a short/variable run, digicut usually wins on total cost and turnaround.

CMYKHub: RouterCut, Kisscut, Drill

The three dieline order types CMYKHub customers can choose from, and what each one expects from the artwork.

1. RouterCut to Dieline

If a customer orders this type of dieline, the artwork is allowed to contain more than one dieline type. It can have Cut, Crease, Drill, and Perf all in one artwork - or only one or two of them.

2. Kisscut to Dieline

This type of dieline is usually used for sticker/label jobs. As we know, kisscut (or "cut-to") requires grip - so when working on a kisscut job, make sure the artboard is larger than the dieline size.

3. Drill to Dieline

Drill in CMYKHub does not literally "drill". It does cut, but not the outer object - it cuts the inner object instead.

White Ink Setup

Sticker / label structure

Surface print

Surface print is commonly used in almost all printing methods. It produces a right-reading result and is intended to be read from the top of the stock.

Reverse print

Reverse print is only used on clear stock, because reading it from the top of the stock produces a wrong-reading result. It's intended to be read from the back of the stock instead - usually used for stickers or labels that stick from the inside rather than the outside of the base.

In the file, white ink should sit on the top layer, and overprint is a must. That's the file setup regardless of print type. On press, though, the actual ink lay-down order is the opposite of the file's layer order for reverse print: white ink lays down first for surface print, and last for reverse print.

White ink on clear stock

White ink is used mostly on clear or metallic substrates.
Unlike CMYK ink, white ink has a special characteristic: it has the strongest density, which makes it opaque.
On clear stock, white ink is mostly used as a background or white base underneath the CMYK print.
CMYK ink without white ink underneath will produce a translucent colour.
If the white ink covers all areas of the label, that can indicate the label requires a reverse print.

White ink on silver / metallic stock

Unlike CMY ink, black and white ink have a special characteristic: their density is strongest, making them even more opaque than on clear stock.
On silver stock, white ink is mostly used as a background or white base underneath the CMYK print.
CMY ink without white ink underneath will produce a translucent metallic colour - some products use this method deliberately to get a metallic colour effect.
Since white ink is opaque, some objects may use white ink only (e.g. text).
If the white ink covers all areas of the label, that might indicate the label requires an opaque stock, to avoid the container colour affecting the stock colour.

Why does white stock still require white ink?

In some cases, white label stock still requires white ink, because of differences in the standard stock's white colour. Even from the same manufacturer, the white of the stock can vary slightly - sometimes it leans yellowish, blueish, or reddish.
To get a standard white colour - especially if the label has a barcode or QR code that's sensitive to its base colour - white ink is needed to establish that standard white as the base.

Barcode & QR

What makes a barcode or QR code scannable in artwork, and the minimum sizes CMYKHub works to.

Barcode anatomy

The parts that make up a 1D barcode: the data character encoded by the pattern, individual bars and spaces, the single narrowest bar/space width (1 pixel/module), the barcode's height, and the quiet zone (margin) that keeps the pattern readable.

Barcode rules

  1. Barcode must use the darkest colour on the artwork.
  2. Avoid using green and/or red for the barcode.
  3. The quiet zone must be wider than any space within the barcode.

QR code anatomy

QR code rules

  1. QR must use the darkest colour on the artwork.
  2. Avoid using green and/or red for the QR code.
  3. The quiet zone must be at least 3 pixels (modules) wide.
Size matters. Since there's no barcode scanner on hand to check barcode or QR grade quality, minimum QR size is 20mm and minimum barcode is 30mm in width - don't go smaller than that.

Folding products

Common brochure fold types, how each one is built, and what each is best suited for.

Half-fold (bifold)

Fold a sheet in half for two panels. A horizontal fold opens like a magazine; a vertical fold gives a conventional commercial brochure. Best for quick business presentations, product guides, and side-by-side product comparisons.

Trifold

Three panels, folded like a letter - when opened, it resembles the letter C. Best for travel brochures, company information, and career fair brochures.

Roll fold

Divide the sheet into four equal parts, fold the rightmost panel inward over the left, then close with the leftmost panel - four panels. Best for step-by-step guides and educational materials.

Z-fold

A type of trifold that moves like an accordion and presents cascading information, three panels. Best for hotel, real estate, and restaurant menu brochures.

4-panel accordion fold

Four equal, alternating panels that open like a fan. Best for healthcare brochures and corporate brochures introducing team members or services.

Gate fold

Two front flaps open outward, building anticipation for what's inside. Best for special events, real estate, and interior decoration brochures.

Double gate fold

Create a classic gate fold, then fold it in half. Best for guides for cinemas, galleries, and art exhibits, year-end reports, and travel brochures.

French fold

Fold the sheet in half vertically, then in half horizontally. This leaves the entire inner panel usable as a poster. Best for beauty and wellness brochures, stylised travel maps, and new product launches.

Half-fold then trifold

Set the sheet vertically, fold it in half top to bottom, then fold it again into three equal columns. Best for art workshops, comprehensive seminars, and pet adoption guides.

Double parallel fold

Fold a half-folded sheet twice, with all panels folding in the same direction. Best for travel guides and conference primers.

General folding and design guidance

  1. Choose the fold based on the story. Multi-panel folds reveal content step by step, while flat brochures show everything at once.
  2. Finalise content before choosing paper size. Content volume determines the size needed - standard sizes range from 297x210mm to 594x420mm, with custom sizes available.
  3. Don't crowd the layout. Multi-panel brochures offer room for images, service listings, and copy, but cluttered designs lose readers quickly.
  4. Keep content off fold lines. Placing objects along creases can affect readability.
  5. Respect margins and bleed. Keep the layout within the margins so images and text boxes aren't cut off.
  6. Do test folds. Practice with a physical sheet at the chosen size, and make a mock-up to plan photo placement, background space, and headline length.
  7. Establish a focal point. Make one element - an image, headline, or pull quote - the page's dominant feature. This matters most on flat brochures.

Photoshop channels

In Photoshop, Spot Channels are the primary channel type used for print production to handle special inks, white underbases, gloss/varnish layers, or custom colour plates beyond standard CMYK.

Key print-related channels in Photoshop

  • CMYK colour channels - Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, and Black (Key). Used for standard full-colour offset, digital, and commercial press printing.
  • Spot channels - added manually via the Channels panel menu (New Spot Channel) to instruct a printer to apply a specific ink or coating to targeted areas:
    • Pantone / custom spot colours - used for exact brand-colour matching on physical presses (e.g. brochures, packaging).
    • White spot ink (W1 / White) - used in DTF, UV printing, or dark-substrate printing to lay down a white underbase or opaque white design layer.
    • Varnish / gloss (W2 / Varnish) - used in specialised UV printers to apply a clear gloss, spot UV, or protective coating layer over specific parts of an image.
  • Alpha channels - used to store custom selections and grayscale masks, which can be converted into spot channels or saved for colour separations in screen printing.

How to create a spot channel for print

  1. Go to Window > Channels to open the Channels panel.
  2. Click the panel menu icon (four horizontal lines) and select New Spot Channel.
  3. Click the colour swatch to pick a visual representation colour (the on-screen colour only affects the display preview, not the actual physical ink colour) and set Solidity (usually 100% for solid inks, or adjusted for preview opacity).
  4. Paint or paste your selection in grayscale on that spot channel - white = 100% ink, black = 0% ink.
  5. Save the document in a print-compatible format that preserves spot data, such as TIFF.
